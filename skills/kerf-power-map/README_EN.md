## What it is

Power Map is a career strategist for people who work in organisations, senior executives included, with the same depth for everyone. When you are stuck between a manager, a peer or another department, or deciding whether to take an offer or change careers, it reads your situation through the frameworks of *Power*, *Getting to Yes* and *The Dictator's Handbook*: who really decides, whom they rely on to stay in place, who cannot do without whom. Then it judges whether this game calls for cooperation, or for facing the power reality and getting hard.

It is on your side but does not flatter you. If your reading rests on thin evidence, is driven by an old grudge, or is so polite it invites the other side to take more, it says so and shows why. The first read of a situation comes with a power map, so you can see the game at a glance instead of digging through paragraphs.

## What you get

The shape of the reply follows what you will use it for, but every reply has:

- **The conclusion**: cooperate or get hard, and why.
- **Where you may be wrong**: a direct challenge to your judgement, with the grounds.
- **The three questions**, as plain-language headings so you can check the reasoning:
  1. Who decides, and whom do they rely on to stay in place?
  2. What do they see, and who cannot do without whom?
  3. In the other side's eyes, what does saying yes or no cost, and what are each side's fallbacks?

  When the decision is in your own hands, such as whether to take an offer or change careers, you are the one deciding, and the questions become: on this path, whom do you rely on, who relies on you, and how real is your fallback? Other people in the picture show up inside those three questions.

The rest depends on what you need:

- **Words you can send, or a path to take**: if you need a message to send tonight, you get the draft and the judgement behind it, including what reading it out commits you to. If you need to decide your next move, you get the chosen strategy and why not the others, what to do in the next two weeks, where it is most likely to fail, and a fallback that is real; a stakeholder table when it helps.
- **A map**: included on the first read; when you only need a message, it may be left out or cut down to the one line that matters.

If you number your questions, it answers them one by one, still opening with the conclusion and the read. When information is missing, it states its assumptions and answers anyway, asking back only about the one or two things that would change the answer. Follow-ups update only the judgements, drafts and map that changed.

## How to ask

Just describe your situation. To give it a full picture in one go, use these five items:

```text
- My role and goal:
- My bottom line, and what I won't do:
- The people involved and how they relate:
- What happened:
- What I have already tried:
```

End with what you want to know; number the questions if there are several. Leave an item blank if you are unsure; it will state its assumptions and answer.

A filled-in example (the short example's question below, rearranged into the five items):

```text
- My role and goal: I have been in Finance for four years. I run the month-end close reports every month, and I am the only one on the team who can. I want to move to the financial analyst opening the New Products team posted last month.
- My bottom line, and what I won't do: Nothing in particular yet.
- The people involved and how they relate: My manager, who has to sign off on internal transfers. Peijun, the New Products manager. HR, who runs the transfer process.
- What happened: HR says an internal transfer needs my current manager's sign-off, and the opening closes at the end of the month.
- What I have already tried: I talked to Peijun. She said, "We'd love to have you, as long as your side lets you go." I raised it with my manager. He said, "If you leave now, who runs the close? Let's talk next year."

How do I get my manager to let me go?
```

## What the map looks like

<div align="center">
  <img src="docs/images/overview.svg" width="800" alt="Power map sketch: three concentric rings with the decision-maker at the centre; one thick blue line is the move to make today; beside it, a small table of what yes and no cost the decision-maker" />
</div>

The shape can change; the way to read it does not. Position carries meaning: the closer to the centre, the more weight someone has with the decision. Nodes hold names only; judgements go on the lines and beside the map. A thick solid line means who cannot do without whom; a thin line shows how word travels. The thick blue line is the one move to make today, and there is only one. Dashed boxes and lines are guesses or things that have not happened yet. Next to the decision-maker sits a small table of what saying yes or no costs them in their own eyes; that table is usually what needs to change. A game with another party is drawn as concentric rings with the decision-maker at the centre; a career decision such as taking an offer is drawn as your position: whom you rely on, who relies on you, how real your fallback is. Maps are saved as HTML in `power-map/` in the working directory.

## The three books

| Book | Authors | Edition | Question it answers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *The Dictator's Handbook* | Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith | 2011 | Who decides, and whom do they rely on |
| *Power: Why Some People Have It and Others Don't* | Jeffrey Pfeffer | 2010 | What they see, and who cannot do without whom |
| *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In* | Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton | 3rd edition (2011) | What yes and no cost, and each side's fallback |

The skill bundles a guide to the books' analytical tools ([books.md](references/books.md)), 269 book stories with full plots ([stories.md](references/stories.md)) and a one-line index of 712 cases ([case-library](references/case-library/index.md)), all in Traditional Chinese. They are summaries written for this skill, not quotations, so you do not need the books. Titles and terms appear only when they bring an insight you would not have reached yourself; there are no book-by-book summaries.

These three books are the only sources. If you name another book, such as *Managing with Power*, it answers from general knowledge and says that the book is outside the skill's sources.

## Install with your agent

Give this to Codex or Claude Code:

```text
Install Power Map (skills/kerf-power-map) from https://github.com/kerfnotes/skills into this project.
Read skills/kerf-power-map/docs/install.md first, pick the install path for the agent in use,
run the checks, then report the install path, source commit and check results.
```

The project path is `.agents/skills/kerf-power-map/` for Codex and `.claude/skills/kerf-power-map/` for Claude Code. The whole `skills/kerf-power-map/` folder is the skill. It is only Markdown and HTML, with no extra tools, models or API keys. The [install guide](docs/install.md) (Traditional Chinese) covers download, what to do if an older version is installed, and the completion checks.

With the [`skills`](https://www.npmjs.com/package/skills) CLI you can also pick from the whole repo:

```sh
npx skills add kerfnotes/skills --list
```

After installing, just describe your situation. Or invoke `$kerf-power-map` in Codex and `/kerf-power-map` in Claude Code.

The analysis rests only on what you tell it and cannot see what others think or what goes on inside your organisation. It is an aid to strategic thinking, not legal, HR or counselling advice.
